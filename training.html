Training & onboarding

Good training makes
things click.

Training isn't about transferring information. It's about building the conditions where people can actually use what they've learned. Here's how I think about that — and what I've built.

My approach

Training that respects the person in the seat

Most bad training has the same problem: it was designed for the organization's comfort, not the learner's reality. It assumes people have time, attention, and context they often don't have. It front-loads policy and buries the practical. It measures completion, not comprehension.

I design training and onboarding around a different set of questions: What does this person actually need to do their job? What's the smallest amount of information that makes the biggest difference? What happens when they're confused at 3pm on a Tuesday with no one to ask?

The answers to those questions produce training that's shorter, clearer, and more useful than most organizations default to — and onboarding experiences that make people feel prepared instead of overwhelmed.

Start with the job, not the content
What does someone actually need to do? Work backward from there, not forward from a content library.
Clarity over completeness
A 20-page handbook nobody reads is worse than a 2-page guide people actually use. Every piece of training should make a decision about what to leave out.
Design for the hard day, not the good one
Training should work when someone is tired, overwhelmed, and two weeks into a new role. If it only works in ideal conditions, it doesn't really work.
Onboarding is culture-setting
The first 30 days aren't just about information transfer. They're the organization's first real chance to demonstrate whether it values the people who just joined it.

Design principles

How I make training decisions

01
Needs before solutions
Training is never the first answer. First question: is this actually a training problem, or a process problem, a tool problem, or a management problem? Build what's actually needed.
02
Learner context matters
Who is this person? What do they already know? What conditions are they learning in? Good training design starts with the learner, not the subject matter expert.
03
Measure what matters
Completion rates are the floor, not the ceiling. The real question is: did this change what people do? Build evaluation in from the start.
04
Build for reuse
Training materials that can't be updated or reused create technical debt. Build modularly, document the logic, and design for the next person who maintains it.
